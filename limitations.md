# Limitations of the Policy

**Research Task 7, Phase B stress test**
Pratham Vasani

This section asks where the Maplewood policy fails. It is not an apology for the
policy. It is an account of what I think I accomplished and what I did not, and I
would rather a district adopt this document knowing its edges than adopt it
believing it covers more than it does.

---

## 1. What a well-intentioned employee does wrong

These are the failures I consider most likely, because they do not require anyone
to misbehave.

**The deadline collision.** Section 7.4 says a deadline is never a reason to skip
review. That provision will be tested on an afternoon when the Communications
Director is out, a translated enrollment notice has to go out before a state
reporting deadline, and an assistant who has produced forty of these before
produces the forty-first without written approval. Nothing bad happens. It
happens again. Within a year the review step is something the office does when it
has time. I have written a policy whose central control is a human approval that
competes directly with the operational pressure that made synthesis attractive in
the first place — and when a control and the reason for using the tool point in
opposite directions, the control usually loses. I do not have a fix for this that
does not require staffing I cannot assume.

**The emergency boundary blurs at the edges.** Section 2.1 prohibits synthetic
voice in safety communication, and I think the core cases are clear. The edges
are not. A bus is ninety minutes late in cold weather — routine or safety? A
norovirus outbreak closes a cafeteria — health notice or administrative? An
employee who genuinely wants to comply will have to decide, under time pressure,
and the policy gives them a category rather than a test. I believe some of these
will be miscategorized as routine, and that the miscategorization will go
unnoticed precisely because nothing goes wrong the first several times.

**Disclosure gets trimmed for a good reason.** Section 4.1 requires three seconds
of spoken disclosure at the head of every file. Someone will cut it from a clip
used in a board presentation because it is redundant in that room, which is true.
That clip will then be shared from the board packet. The policy anticipates this
in 4.3 and prohibits it, but it is the kind of prohibition that reads as
pedantic in the moment, and pedantic rules are the ones people quietly set aside.

**Internal prototypes escape.** Section 1.5 permits synthetic voice for internal
drafting and requires deletion afterward. Draft files live in shared drives, get
attached to emails, and survive. A prototype with no disclosure — because it was
never meant to leave — is the single most likely source of an undisclosed
district artifact in circulation. I permitted this use because forbidding it
would be unrealistic, and I think that was the right call, but it is a hole I
opened deliberately and I want to name it as such.

**Consent drifts in scope.** Section 3.4 makes consent narrow, and I expect that
to erode. An employee who consented for translated enrollment messages will be
asked whether their voice can be used for the registration reminder — nearly the
same thing, obviously fine, and a new form feels bureaucratic. Scope creep in
consent does not look like a violation at any single step. The quarterly register
review in 3.8 catches expired consents but would not catch this, because the
record would still show a valid consent; what changed is the use, not the form.

## 2. What a bad-faith actor does anyway

The policy's limits against deliberate misuse are more fundamental, and I want to
be blunt about them.

**Nothing in this policy constrains anyone outside the district.** This is the
largest limitation and it is structural rather than fixable. My Phase A
hypotheticals were mostly external: a booster fabricating a clip, an operative
generating four hundred personalized voicemails before a board election. The
Maplewood policy has nothing to say to any of them. It governs our production. It
does not govern the thing that actually threatens us. A district could comply
perfectly and still have a fabricated superintendent announcement circulating in
parent group chats by Tuesday.

What the policy does provide is narrower and worth stating accurately: because of
the production log in 5.3 and the master copies in 5.4, the district can say
quickly and specifically that a given clip is not ours. That is a real capability
and most organizations do not have it. It is a forensic capability, not a
preventive one. It helps in hour six, not hour zero, and by hour six a false
emergency message has already done what it was going to do.

**An insider defeats this policy easily.** Every control here assumes the
employee wants to comply. An employee who does not can generate audio on a
personal laptop in seventeen seconds using free local tools that require no
account, no API key, and leave no record anywhere. My Task 6 work established
this empirically rather than theoretically. Nothing in the policy detects it,
because there is nothing to detect — the capability does not route through any
system the district administers.

**The prohibitions are the only load-bearing parts.** I have come to think the
procedural sections of this policy — review, provenance, logging — are mostly
useful for preventing accidents, and that the genuinely protective provisions are
the flat refusals in Section 2. A refusal does not depend on a reviewer being
available, a log being checked, or a tool behaving as documented. It just removes
a category of use. If I had to cut this policy to one page, I would keep Section
2 and Section 4.1 and lose almost everything else without losing most of the
protection.

## 3. Where the policy relies on good faith, and whether that is fatal

It relies on good faith nearly everywhere. Employees self-report under 8.1.
Reviewers apply judgment under 6.4. Consent is honored because the office honors
it. The Communications Director's discretionary refusal under 7.6 has no appeal
and no criterion.

Task 7 asks whether that reliance is a fatal weakness or an honest one. My answer
is that it is honest for internal conduct and fatal for external threat, and that
the mistake would be to let the first reassure us about the second.

Internally, good faith is not a weakness but the only thing available. Any policy
governing 1,100 people runs on their willingness to follow it; the alternative is
surveillance that would cost more than the harm it prevents and would poison the
working relationship the consent provisions depend on. Section 8.1's amnesty for
prompt self-reporting is my attempt to make good faith easier than concealment,
which I think is the most a policy can do here.

Externally, good faith is irrelevant, and a district that reads this policy and
feels protected has misread it. The policy addresses our production. Our
production was never the main risk. I think that gap is unclosable at the
district level — it is a platform, regulatory, and detection problem — and I
would rather say so than add a section that gestures at it convincingly.

## 4. What I am not sure about

**Whether Section 2.1 is too absolute.** I prohibited synthetic voice in all
emergency communication without exception. There is a real counterargument: a
genuine emergency at 2 a.m. in a district where the on-call administrator speaks
only English, with families who need the message in Somali or Karen immediately,
is a case where synthesis might save someone from harm. I considered a narrow
exception and decided against it, on the grounds that an exception invoked under
maximum time pressure by a frightened person is an exception that will be
stretched. I am not confident that was right. It may be that I protected the
channel's credibility at the cost of a family who did not get a message they
could understand.

**Whether the in-signal disclosure actually works on listeners.** Section 4.1 is
the provision I am most confident about technically — a spoken line survives
re-encoding and forwarding in a way nothing else does. I have no evidence it
survives *attention*. Families who receive weekly district calls will learn the
opening three seconds are boilerplate and stop hearing them within a month. I may
have solved the technical durability problem and left the human one untouched,
and I do not know how to test that from where I sit.

**Whether a total student prohibition is correct.** Section 2.2 refuses all
student voice replication with no consent pathway. I believe this is right. But a
student who wants to use voice synthesis in their own media-literacy project is
being refused a legitimate educational experience — arguably the exact experience
Task 6 gave me, and which this entire analysis depends on having had. I have
written a policy that forbids the students in my chosen district from doing the
assignment that taught me what I know. I noticed this only while drafting this
section, and I have not resolved it.

## 5. What this policy would not have prevented

A final test, applied to my own Phase A hypotheticals.

| Scenario | Prevented? |
| --- | --- |
| Booster fabricates audio about the program | No. External actor, no district control applies. |
| Cloned administrator voice used against the district | No. Detection and response only, after the fact. |
| My own disclosed artifact circulating stripped of context | **Partly.** In-signal disclosure under 4.1 survives the forwarding and re-encoding that defeated the filename. |
| Four hundred personalized voicemails before a board election | No. The district cannot act on this at all. |
| Internal prototype escaping undisclosed | **Partly.** 1.5 requires deletion; nothing enforces it. |
| Employee voice reused outside consented scope | **Yes,** if the register review catches it, which it may not. |

Two and a half out of six, with the clearest wins on accidents and the clearest
losses on attacks. That distribution is not an accident of drafting — it follows
from the conclusion I reached in the Phase A mitigation survey, that these
controls work against carelessness and fail against adversaries. The policy
reflects that honestly. I would rather hand a superintendent a document that
protects against the half of the problem it can reach, with the other half named
plainly, than one that reads as comprehensive and produces the complacency this
task warns about.
